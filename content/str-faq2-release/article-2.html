An Airbnb renovation budget is not automatically a depreciation schedule. Before a cost segregation study assigns recovery periods to capitalized property, the preparer needs to evaluate whether particular spending is currently deductible, capitalized or subject to an applicable election or safe harbor. A bookkeeping label such as “repair,” “refresh” or “upgrade” does not decide that question.

The distinction matters because an owner should not pay for a component allocation on costs that have already been treated correctly elsewhere, or count the same invoice as both an expense and depreciable basis. It also matters when several projects are bundled into one contractor bill.

This guide explains how to organize repair and improvement records for an STR, what questions the IRS tangible property guidance raises, and how to connect the conclusion to a study without turning every guest-ready purchase into an automatic deduction.

Start with the work, not the invoice heading

Describe what condition existed before the work and what changed afterward. Identify the affected property, the scope, the reason for the work and the relevant dates. A vendor may use “maintenance” to describe a major replacement, while a manager may use “capital” for spending the preparer needs to examine more closely.

The IRS tangible property guidance evaluates improvements through betterment, restoration and adaptation concepts, applied to the relevant unit of property and, for buildings, the appropriate structure or system. A cost amount alone does not resolve those questions.

This means the useful first request is often for scope detail. “Repaired leak in specified location” provides different evidence from “replaced the entire identified system.” Neither description should be converted into a tax conclusion without the applicable review.

Identify the relevant property and building system

An owner sees one rental listing, but the tax analysis may distinguish building systems, equipment and other property. Ask the preparer which unit of property matters for the spending being reviewed. The answer can affect how the scope is evaluated.

Provide before-and-after information and itemized work descriptions. If a project involves plumbing, electrical work and room finishes, identify each part rather than summarizing the total as one repair. Keep the contractor's original scope and any change orders so the final description reflects what was actually done.

The contractor invoice guide and component evidence review explain how to gather this detail. They are organizing tools, not rules that classify work solely by trade name.

A refresh-budget example

Assume a hypothetical owner spends $18,000 preparing an operating Airbnb for the next season. The file contains $2,000 of servicing, $5,000 of furniture purchases, $8,000 of building work and $3,000 of installation and related charges. The amounts total $18,000.

Those categories are a request for further analysis, not a completed tax allocation. What did the servicing address? Which furniture items were acquired and owned? What building work occurred? Which costs belong with which assets? Were items reimbursed or already recorded on another schedule?

The owner should not deduct the entire $18,000 as repairs and then give the same total to the study team as new capital basis. Nor should the owner assume that calling the project an “upgrade” requires every dollar to receive the same treatment. The preparer's reconciliation should connect each accepted treatment to the underlying spending.

Can the de minimis safe harbor help?

The IRS guidance describes a de minimis safe harbor with conditions, including applicable dollar ceilings, accounting procedures and an election. The commonly discussed ceilings are not a rule that every low-dollar rental purchase is automatically deductible or every higher-dollar cost must be capitalized.

For a taxpayer without an applicable financial statement, the guidance does not universally require written accounting procedures, but it does address consistent expensing procedures or policy in place at the beginning of the year. Do not create a backdated policy because an internet checklist says one is always required.

Use the safe-harbor comparison guide to frame the separate election question. The preparer should confirm current requirements for the taxpayer and filing year before the owner treats a threshold as permission to expense an entire project.

Repairs and cost segregation solve different problems

A repair-versus-capitalization review determines how spending should enter the tax records. Cost segregation supports the classification and allocation of relevant depreciable costs. One does not eliminate the need for the other.

If a cost is capitalized, the provider needs the accepted basis and supporting scope. If a cost is properly expensed or treated under an applicable safe harbor, it should not also be included as new depreciable basis. Maintain a clear bridge between the accountant's project ledger and the study inputs.

The report acceptance guide helps identify the questions to resolve before implementation. A report should not silently replace the preparer's capitalization conclusion with a vendor's category or a generic component percentage.

What if the work replaced an old asset?

New spending and the old asset's treatment require separate attention. Identify what was removed, when it was removed and how it appears on the prior schedule. The cost of today's replacement is not automatically the remaining basis of the old property.

A partial disposition question may arise, but an owner should not assume every replacement produces an immediate additional write-off. Election timing, the asset involved and supported basis matter. The partial disposition guide explains that separate handoff.

Keep old and new records together without combining their costs. A clean file explains which asset remains, which was removed and what the replacement project added.

Repairs and improvements FAQ

Does a large invoice always mean an improvement?

Not by price alone. The work and applicable rules matter. Give the preparer the scope and condition history rather than assuming a dollar amount determines capitalization.

Does a small invoice always mean an immediate deduction?

No. Evaluate the facts and any applicable safe harbor or election. Avoid breaking a real project into unsupported labels simply to make individual amounts look smaller.

Can I rely on my manager's accounting category?

Use it as a starting point for reconciliation. Underlying invoices and work descriptions may be necessary for the tax review. A management software category is not itself a federal classification conclusion.

Does work done before the first guest always count as a repair?

The timing and purpose of acquisition and preparation work need review. Do not assume all pre-opening work is deductible merely because the contractor used the word “repair.” Keep the readiness timeline and project scope together.

Can a single renovation contain different treatments?

Yes, the review should examine the actual spending rather than force every item into one category. The important requirement for the owner file is a supported reconciliation that prevents omission and duplication.

Is new furniture the same as a building repair?

Describe it as the asset acquired and preserve the invoice. The preparer can then evaluate expensing and depreciation under the relevant rules. See the STR furnishing guide for the acquisition and replacement records.

Does capitalizing a cost guarantee bonus depreciation?

No. Capitalization does not establish the asset's recovery period or bonus eligibility. Classification, acquisition facts, service dates, elections and other requirements still need review.

Close the project ledger before the study is finalized

Prepare a schedule of invoices, credits, reimbursements and accepted treatments. Identify each source once, and mark open questions for the appropriate professional. Ask the study team which capital costs are included and how they reconcile to the approved total.

Preserve service-date evidence for later improvements rather than assigning every invoice the building's original date. If a project was completed in phases, use the renovation timeline guide to distinguish the events.

A final review should ask whether every material cost has a supported treatment, whether any amount appears twice and whether the asset ledger reflects removals. Resolve discrepancies before turning an estimate into a return entry.

Resolve mixed invoices without rewriting history

Keep the original invoice and request a factual supplement when its scope is unclear. The contractor can explain what work occurred without being asked to supply a tax classification. Match the supplement to the original total and identify any change orders or credits.

Do not replace an inconvenient description with a new label that changes the substance of the work. A useful supplement explains quantities, locations and tasks. The preparer then records the accepted treatment and the study team uses the relevant capital-cost inputs. That separation makes the file easier to review when the owner changes providers or preparers later.
