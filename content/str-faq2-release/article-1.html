A hot tub or pool can be an important part of an Airbnb's guest experience, but the amenity's name does not establish its depreciation treatment. A cost segregation review needs to identify what the owner acquired or built, how it functions, who owns it and which costs belong to it. The answer is not a universal promise that every outdoor amenity is five-year or fifteen-year property.

The practical starting point is a project file. A portable unit, a built-in installation, site construction and building work can involve different facts. A single invoice labeled “backyard upgrade” often hides those differences. Better records help the provider and preparer evaluate the actual assets without inventing a percentage from listing photographs.

This guide answers the questions an STR owner should resolve before putting hot tub, pool or related outdoor costs into a study projection.

Can a hot tub or pool be included in a cost segregation review?

It can be reviewed as part of the relevant property costs, but inclusion in the review is not a classification conclusion. Start with the owner, supported cost and intended rental use. An amenity owned by an association or supplied under a lease is a different question from an asset the rental owner purchased.

The provider should explain the classification basis for the specific property. IRS Publication 946 discusses recovery periods, asset classes and land improvements. It also distinguishes depreciation from section 179 eligibility. A shorter recovery period, where supported, does not make every expensing rule available.

For the broader framework, read the component evidence guide and land improvement review. This article applies those questions to an amenity project rather than assigning a class from a shopping receipt alone.

Separate the equipment from the project around it

A project can include the tub or pool, delivery, installation, electrical work, plumbing, paving, enclosure work and other construction. Do not assume all of those costs have one treatment because they were purchased together.

Ask the contractor to describe the scope and identify costs that can be supported separately. Record how the installation connects to the building and what each system serves. Photographs before, during and after work can help explain facts that the final guest listing does not show.

Do not create an unsupported split simply because more categories look favorable. The total still needs to reconcile to the approved project cost. The purpose of detail is evidence, not a larger count of accelerated assets.

A cost-reconciliation example

Assume an owner has a hypothetical $24,000 project: $10,000 for an amenity unit, $3,000 for delivery and installation, $5,000 for supporting electrical work and $6,000 for surrounding site work. Those amounts total $24,000.

The example does not assign a recovery period to any line. Instead, it shows the questions that should be asked. What does installation include? Is the electrical work dedicated to the equipment or serving a broader area? What was actually built in the site-work portion? Were any charges reimbursed, duplicated or included elsewhere?

A report that allocates $24,000 here and also includes the same invoices in a building renovation total would duplicate costs. A report that calls all $24,000 “five-year equipment” without addressing the supporting work would skip the classification evidence. Both problems can be detected before the return is prepared.

What changes when the amenity came with the property?

A seller's listing may show a pool, spa or hot tub, but an asking-price description is not a separate tax basis schedule. The acquisition needs a supported allocation among the assets acquired. Later replacement spending is a different event and should not silently replace the original purchase allocation.

Keep the purchase documents, seller inventory and any appraisal material relevant to the amenity. Identify what existed at acquisition and what the owner added later. Current photographs may not show the original installation, particularly if a new model or major renovation has replaced it.

The seller furniture allocation guide and land value guide explain the separate acquisition questions. Avoid choosing a retail replacement price and treating it automatically as the amount paid for an older acquired asset.

Ownership matters for condos and managed properties

A pool may be an association amenity rather than an asset owned directly by the unit owner. A property manager may provide equipment under an arrangement that needs clarification. An owner may pay an assessment without purchasing a separately identifiable pool.

Send the relevant ownership or association documents to the preparer. Explain who owns the amenity, who paid for the work and what the payment represents. Do not assume that an amenity available to guests belongs in the individual's component inventory.

Use the condo common-area guide when the property's boundaries are unclear. A study can only work with the ownership and basis facts established for the engagement.

When does a later amenity enter service?

The building's closing date, first booking and later amenity completion may be different. Keep delivery, installation, completion and availability records for the specific project. Payment alone is not a universal service-date conclusion.

Suppose the home was already accepting guests when an outdoor project began. Record the existing rental's history and the later project's milestones separately. Do not move the whole property's date forward or assign the old date to every new cost.

The phased-opening guide provides an event-ledger approach. The preparer should evaluate the supported dates and relevant rules before a first-year deduction estimate is finalized.

Does the amenity automatically qualify for bonus depreciation?

No. Classification is one input. The applicable acquisition and service facts, depreciation system, elections and other eligibility rules also need review. A sales claim that an amenity is “a write-off” does not establish treatment for the purchaser's property and tax year.

Ask the provider to identify assumptions in any preliminary estimate. Then ask the preparer how the resulting depreciation interacts with rental use, loss limitations and state treatment. Even a supported deduction does not establish that the owner can use the full amount against wages now.

The bonus depreciation guide and loss limitation review address those separate decisions.

Hot tub and pool FAQ

Is a portable hot tub always five-year property?

Do not treat portability as a complete classification analysis. Provide ownership, function, installation and activity facts. Ask for the applicable class and supporting reasoning rather than relying on a general internet label.

Is every swimming pool fifteen-year property?

No universal label should replace the asset-class review. Publication 946 discusses land improvements and activity-based classifications. Identify the actual property and use, and confirm the applicable depreciation system with the preparer.

Can I use the contractor's replacement estimate as the old asset's basis?

An estimate for new work does not automatically establish the cost or remaining basis of the old asset. Preserve the acquisition and depreciation history. Replacing an amenity requires a separate review of the removed property as well as the new project.

Does routine servicing belong in the study's capital-cost pool?

Have the preparer evaluate the spending rather than adding every maintenance payment to the asset basis. Operating service, repairs, replacement equipment and larger improvements can raise different questions. Keep the invoice detail so those questions can be answered.

What if guests and my family both use the amenity?

Provide the property's personal-use records and relevant asset-use facts. A separate amenity classification does not remove the need to review rental and personal use. The vacation-rental guide explains that handoff.

What happens if the tub is removed or replaced later?

Tell the preparer which asset was removed and when, and retain the replacement records. Do not leave the old asset in the inventory without explanation or deduct its basis twice. See the partial disposition guide.

A practical owner checklist

Prepare one folder for the acquisition or project, with an index showing the invoice, payer, affected asset, date and unresolved question. Add photographs and plans that explain the installation. Connect the accepted cost to the report and current asset ledger.

A good next step is to resolve missing ownership or scope facts before asking for a precise savings estimate. Stratum studies cost $1 per square foot, with a $2,000 minimum per property. Confirm any separately engaged advisory or filing work, and discuss your property and study scope using the actual project records.

Compare proposals using the same amenity records

If two providers give different preliminary answers, send them the same installation description and cost reconciliation before comparing the estimates. One may have assumed a portable unit while the other assumed an integrated construction project. A disagreement in inputs can look like a difference in expertise or tax benefit.

Ask each provider which costs are included, which facts remain unverified and what additional evidence is required. Retain the written response with the proposal. An estimate should become more specific as the evidence improves, rather than remaining an unexplained percentage of the home's value.
