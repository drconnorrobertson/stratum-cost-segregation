Can You Depreciate an Airbnb Before the First Guest Arrives?

The first paid booking is not the only fact that matters when determining an Airbnb's depreciation start date. IRS Publication 527 ties rental service to when property is ready and available for its specific rental use. An empty but genuinely available rental presents a different question from a property still under construction or missing essential equipment.

For an STR owner, the practical challenge is proving the timeline. Closing, furniture delivery, listing publication, local approvals and the first guest stay may fall on different dates. A cost segregation study should use supported facts rather than whichever milestone produces the desired tax year.

This guide explains how to organize that evidence, how to handle staggered assets, and why the service-date question must remain separate from bonus eligibility and loss usability.

Start with the property's actual readiness

A public listing can be useful evidence, but a listing by itself does not settle whether the home was ready and available for the intended use. Ask what a guest could actually have rented on the claimed date. Record unresolved construction, missing facilities, blocked availability and operational restrictions rather than omitting them from the file.

The same discipline applies in the opposite direction. A property may have been ready and available before someone booked it. A slow first month does not necessarily mean the rental had not entered service. The owner's file should explain what was offered and when, not rely entirely on the booking platform's first payment timestamp.

Publication 527 includes examples distinguishing readiness from later rental occupancy. Use those examples as orientation, then have the preparer apply the rule to the property's facts. They are not permission to backdate a launch.

Build a timeline with several kinds of evidence

An effective timeline is short enough to read and detailed enough to verify. Record the event, date, source document and person who can explain it. Avoid a reconstructed story with no supporting references.

Useful records may include completion documents, installation invoices, dated photographs, communications with the manager, rental advertisements and calendar availability. If local authorization affects the ability to offer the intended rental, include the relevant record and ask the adviser how it affects the analysis.

Do not send every document as an unlabelled attachment. A single index connecting “kitchen installation complete” to an invoice, photograph and manager email is easier to evaluate. Mark a disagreement between records as an open question.

The STR document register provides a starting format for this handoff.

A year-end example with two possible outcomes

Assume an owner closes on a cabin in November, publishes photographs in December and welcomes a first guest in January. These hypothetical dates do not establish a December service date.

In one version, required work is complete, the cabin is equipped, and guests can genuinely reserve and use it in December. In another, the listing is visible but the calendar is blocked while essential construction continues into January. The first stay occurs in January in both versions, yet the underlying readiness facts differ.

The preparer's job is to evaluate the evidence. The owner and study provider should not collapse both versions into “we had a listing before December 31.” Nor should they automatically conclude that every asset entered service on the first guest's arrival.

A useful report handoff records the accepted date, the evidence supporting it and any assets that require separate treatment.

Furniture and improvements may have different dates

A property is not necessarily one undivided service-date event. Later furniture purchases, a newly completed amenity or a phased project can require separate review. The date an invoice is paid may differ from delivery, installation and readiness for use.

For example, a newly ordered appliance might be delivered while the rental is otherwise operating but remain uninstalled. Its purchase record helps establish cost; the installation and readiness records answer a different question. Do not use the operating home's earlier date to erase the new asset's timeline.

The furnishing guide and phased-opening guide help separate these events. A cost segregation report should connect each relevant input to the appropriate property or project history.

Availability, personal use and activity classification are different questions

An owner may have several tax questions at launch. When did property enter service? Was it used personally? How is the activity classified under the passive rules? Can the owner use the projected depreciation loss? Those questions interact, but one answer does not substitute for the others.

An owner stay during setup needs an accurate description. A blocked calendar needs a reason. A guest inquiry is not necessarily a completed rental day. Keep source records that allow the preparer to distinguish the facts instead of assigning all non-booked dates to one convenient category.

For a vacation home with owner stays, consult the vacation-rental guide. For activity and participation questions, use the STR strategy review.

Does year-end readiness guarantee bonus depreciation?

No. A supported service date is one input. Acquisition requirements, asset classification, elections, applicable depreciation system and other eligibility rules still need review. The study's completion date does not make every component newly acquired or change an earlier service date.

A year-end estimate also needs to account for conventions where relevant. The mid-quarter guide explains why the preparer may need information about other applicable additions, beyond the one Airbnb in the study.

If the property's launch is uncertain, ask for a scenario analysis showing which assumptions remain unresolved. A projection should name its assumptions rather than presenting an unsupported year-end deduction as a fact.

FAQ: no bookings, blocked calendars and late deliveries

Does no rental income mean no depreciation?

Not automatically. The readiness and availability facts matter. Separately, the preparer must evaluate the property's use and the return treatment; absence of income alone does not complete that analysis.

Does creating an Airbnb listing establish the service date?

It is evidence, not a universal answer. Keep the listing date alongside completion, equipment and availability records. Explain any mismatch instead of selecting one document in isolation.

What if the calendar was blocked for my own trip?

Record the reason and actual use. Do not describe owner-blocked dates as available rental dates merely because the listing remained online. Personal-use treatment requires its own review.

What if I bought furniture in December but installed it in January?

Preserve both records and ask the preparer to determine the asset's service date. A payment date does not automatically establish readiness. Avoid using a building-level date for every later purchase.

Can I commission a study before the property opens?

Ask the provider what work can be performed, which inputs are provisional and how final service-date evidence will be incorporated. Do not assume a preliminary report establishes the eventual tax treatment.

Can a later study change the year I actually opened?

A later report should reflect supported history. If prior filings used a treatment that may need correction, the preparer must assess the appropriate procedure. The Form 3115 guide covers that separate decision.

What to send before approving the report

Prepare a dated launch timeline, an indexed evidence folder and a list of unresolved assets. Identify the person responsible for confirming the final dates with the preparer. Ask the study team to distinguish verified facts from assumptions in the deliverable.

Resolve conflicting launch records before the report is final

Suppose the manager's email says the home was ready on December 18, a contractor's completion document is dated January 4, and the first unblocked calendar date is January 10. Those records may describe different scopes, or they may expose a real inconsistency. The owner should ask what each document refers to rather than deleting the inconvenient dates.

Create a short reconciliation note. Identify which work remained, whether it affected the specific intended use, who can confirm the facts and what additional evidence exists. The preparer can then evaluate a coherent account rather than three unexplained timestamps.

If only one later amenity was incomplete, explain that distinction. If essential facilities were unavailable, explain that too. Do not assume every unfinished item delays every asset, or that no unfinished item could matter. The facts should drive the analysis.

Once the date is approved, keep the conclusion with the evidence and tell both the provider and preparer which version is final. A later spreadsheet revision should not silently replace a previously reviewed date. Version control matters even for a small property file.

This process is particularly useful when the study is ordered after the launch year. The owner's memory may be clear about the first guest but less clear about readiness several weeks earlier. An indexed record trail makes the historical review more reliable without turning a general rule into a retrospective guarantee.
