A mortgage does not, by itself, prevent an Airbnb owner from evaluating cost segregation. The starting point is the supported tax basis of property the taxpayer owns, not simply the down payment. Financing, depreciation classification and the ability to use a deduction are separate decisions.

That distinction matters when an owner compares two purchases with the same price but different financing. The cash buyer and the financed buyer have different debt and cash-flow positions. They do not necessarily have different acquisition costs merely because one borrowed more. Neither should estimate a study benefit by multiplying a down payment by an advertised reclassification percentage.

This guide explains how to prepare a financed STR acquisition for a study, what the mortgage documents establish, and which questions belong with the tax preparer before a deduction enters a projection.

What is the difference between down payment and property basis?

The down payment is part of the funding used to complete a purchase. Basis is a tax measurement that begins with the applicable acquisition rules and is adjusted for relevant items. IRS Publication 551 explains that purchased-property cost can include cash and debt obligations. It does not say an owner may depreciate only the cash contributed at closing.

For a rental acquisition, the preparer must then determine how the supported cost is allocated. Land is not depreciable. Building costs, transferred furnishings and other acquired assets require their own treatment. Closing adjustments also need review; a lender's total cash-to-close figure is not automatically the depreciable basis.

A useful project file therefore contains two reconciliations. One explains how the transaction was funded. The other explains the tax basis and its allocation. They should connect to the same closing documents without being mistaken for the same calculation.

A financed-purchase example without a tax-saving promise

Assume an owner purchases a furnished rental for $800,000 using $200,000 of cash and $600,000 of acquisition financing. These are hypothetical funding amounts, not an allocation or a tax result.

The useful first observation is that the $200,000 down payment is not the same thing as the $800,000 contract price. The next step is to reconcile the closing statement, identify land and acquired assets, and determine the relevant basis. The example deliberately does not assign a land percentage, furnishing value or accelerated deduction. Those figures require evidence.

Suppose a seller separately describes furniture in the purchase agreement. That description is a starting record, not an instruction to count the same value twice. The provider and preparer need to reconcile any supported furnishing allocation with the remaining property costs.

A complete projection would also review when the assets entered service, applicable depreciation rules, prior elections where relevant and owner-level loss limits. Financing numbers alone answer none of those questions.

Which mortgage and closing documents should you send?

Start with the executed closing statement, purchase agreement and relevant allocation schedules. Include the appraisal if it provides useful acquisition-date information, but identify its purpose and date. A lender appraisal should not be treated as a complete cost segregation report.

Provide a clear ownership record as well. The borrower name, title holder and tax owner may require explanation if different entities or people appear in the file. Resolve that question before the provider prepares a report for the wrong taxpayer.

Keep loan charges separate from asset acquisition costs while the preparer determines their treatment. Avoid sending a single spreadsheet column labeled “all closing costs” with no supporting detail. A total that mixes lender expenses, tax adjustments, deposits and property costs makes the handoff harder to review.

If furnishings were bought after closing, provide those invoices separately. Their acquisition and service dates may differ from the building's. Use the STR document checklist to record what is available and what remains unresolved.

Does paying mortgage principal create another depreciable cost?

A mortgage payment should not simply be added to the study's cost pool every month. Doing so can duplicate acquisition costs already reflected in basis. Keep debt principal, financing expenses, operating expenses and new capital spending in separate records for the preparer.

If an owner later buys a new HVAC system or furnishes an additional room, that project needs its own cost evidence. Its treatment does not come from the fact that the owner used borrowed funds to pay the invoice. Conversely, a principal payment is not a new physical improvement merely because money left the operating account.

This is a bookkeeping handoff question worth resolving early. A clean ledger lets the preparer trace the acquisition once, then review later events on their own facts.

What if the lender's appraisal and assessor disagree?

Do not select whichever figure produces the largest deduction. Record both documents, their dates and what each actually measures. Ask the preparer which evidence supports the acquisition allocation and how material differences should be resolved.

A cost segregation provider should be able to work from an approved basis reconciliation and explain its component allocations. The provider's construction estimates and the lender's collateral analysis serve different purposes. Neither should silently overwrite the owner's tax records.

For a condo or townhouse, also identify ownership boundaries. An amenity appearing in a lender appraisal or listing may belong to an association rather than the individual unit owner. The mortgage does not transform association property into a personally owned depreciable asset.

Does leverage determine whether an STR loss offsets wages?

No simple financing ratio establishes that result. The preparer must separately review the activity, participation, owner basis, amounts at risk and other applicable limitations. A study's component schedule is not a conclusion that a resulting loss offsets W-2 income.

This matters for highly financed acquisitions because a large projected depreciation amount can look compelling next to a relatively small cash contribution. The owner still needs a usable-benefit calculation, realistic implementation costs and a holding-period review.

Mortgage FAQ for Airbnb owners

Can I order a study before the loan is paid off?

A paid-off loan is not a universal prerequisite. Confirm ownership, basis and the engagement scope instead. Tell the provider about unresolved title or transaction questions before work begins.

Does a larger down payment produce a larger study deduction?

Not automatically. A down payment changes financing. The study works with supported property costs and classifications. The preparer evaluates how the resulting depreciation reaches the return.

Can a lender's furniture allowance become the study allocation?

Not without review. Identify what the allowance represents, whether the assets were acquired, who owns them and how the amount connects to the transaction. Avoid treating an underwriting estimate as conclusive tax support.

Should a cash buyer skip this review?

No. Cash purchases still need basis allocation, service-date evidence and loss-usability analysis. Removing a mortgage does not remove those steps.

What if I refinanced after buying the Airbnb?

Keep the original purchase and later refinancing records separate. Explain how any new funds were used rather than changing the acquisition price to the new loan balance. The refinancing guide addresses that separate event and should be reviewed with current records.

Does a study include reviewing my loan structure?

Confirm the written engagement. A component study and individualized tax or financing advice are different services. Do not infer that every related question is included in a study fee.

A practical pre-study handoff

Send the provider an organized acquisition folder and an approved basis summary. Ask the preparer to identify unresolved ownership, allocation and loss-limit questions. Then request a proposal that names the property, deliverables, evidence requirements and implementation responsibilities.

Stratum studies cost $1 per square foot, with a $2,000 minimum per property. Confirm any separately engaged tax advisory or filing work when comparing the full cost. A financed owner should evaluate that fee using supported usable benefits rather than leverage-based marketing arithmetic.

How to review the estimate before spending money

Ask for an estimate that starts with a labeled cost input, not an unexplained property value. Identify whether the figure is a contract price, approved depreciable basis or preliminary number awaiting the land allocation. This prevents a percentage estimate from appearing more final than it is.

Next, ask what assumptions are being made about furnishings, later renovations and service dates. A financed acquisition with substantial post-closing work may need more than the closing statement. Keep that work separate so the provider can explain which records support each part of the estimate.

Then ask the preparer to review the owner-side assumptions. A proposed depreciation amount should not become an expected refund merely because the owner contributed a smaller down payment. Compare the usable timing benefit, financing cash flow, study fee, filing costs and expected sale as separate lines.

Finally, record what would change the decision. If missing basis records, a near-term sale or limited loss usability makes the projection uncertain, agree on the evidence needed before proceeding. A useful proposal remains useful when it identifies a reason to wait.

This review also improves provider comparisons. Send the same approved inputs to each provider and ask each to describe scope, open questions and deliverables. Comparing a purchase-price estimate from one firm with a basis-based estimate from another can create a misleading appearance of different performance.
