Hot tubs|For a spa, the cabinet and its equipment can be photographed directly, but the supporting construction often needs a separate contractor record. Ask whether delivery included a crane, whether the base was newly constructed, and whether the electrical circuit was part of another remodel. A replacement spa can fit on the same deck and use the same service, so a current photograph may conceal substantial retained work. An inventory should identify the particular unit rather than use a permanent line called backyard amenities.
Swimming pools|A pool drawing can help separate the basin perimeter, paved area, equipment location, and surrounding barriers. Request quantities for the actual paving rather than using the entire backyard area. Equipment installed later may have its own invoice and date, while the shell appears only in the acquisition history. Water features and seating may belong to the same contractor package without serving the same function. Ask about common ownership when a condominium or neighborhood association controls the pool visible in listing photographs.
Pool heaters|Photograph the model label and the inlet and outlet connections. The rating and installation instructions can help explain what the unit serves without requiring the owner to infer a tax life. A fuel upgrade may include work beyond the heater, especially when the same gas supply also serves a range or fireplace. Obtain the installer's description of that shared work. If the heater was purchased used, retain acquisition evidence rather than estimating its cost from a new retail listing.
Saunas|A manufacturer's assembly guide can show whether panels form a freestanding enclosure or require construction of a dedicated room. Capture the floor beneath the kit and the electrical connection where safely visible. Ventilation, insulation, and waterproofing may have been included in a wider bathroom contract. A supplier's sauna package description may omit those expenses. If an owner uses the sauna personally between guest stays, explain that use to the preparer rather than assuming that placement in a rental establishes exclusive business use.
Outdoor showers|Trace the fixture to the water supply and drainage destination using a contractor sketch. Identify whether the enclosure rests on a constructed slab, attaches to the house, or consists of removable screening. Keep any work on shared domestic water lines visible in the breakdown. A plumber may group the outdoor shower with a bathroom project, but the report still needs location and function. Photographs should distinguish the shower head and valves from the enclosure, floor, and surrounding privacy landscaping.
Fire pits|The fuel connection and supporting base are often more informative than the finished appearance. A portable bowl may sit on a masonry patio without becoming part of the patio construction. Conversely, an installed fire feature can include concealed gas work and permanent seats. Ask whether a quote includes the fuel unit or only surrounding hardscape. Retain a count of movable chairs separately. For replacement work, distinguish replacing a burner from rebuilding the entire masonry assembly or changing the adjacent paved area.
Outdoor kitchens|List each appliance and identify whether its invoice includes freight or installation. Photograph cabinet attachment, counter support, and utility entry points. The shelter above the cooking area may have its own foundations and framing even if it was marketed as part of the kitchen. Ask the contractor to distinguish new utility lines from modifications to existing house services. If the seller supplied loose grilling equipment, reconcile its bill of sale before attributing acquired building costs to the same items.
Pergolas|Useful drawings show post locations, foundation details, beams, house attachment, and any movable roof covering. Clarify whether electrical work serves only the structure or extends building circuits elsewhere. A manufacturer kit invoice may omit excavation, footings, and contractor assembly. These missing items should not be inferred from a package price without explanation. When replacing fabric or controls, preserve the distinction from the retained posts and beams. The study description should match the constructed structure rather than repeat the marketing term patio accessory.
Decks|Measure the original and expanded footprints separately where the renovation changed the platform. Capture ledger-board attachment and the support layout from a safe accessible position. A deck invoice may include stairs, drainage repairs, railings, and replacement of only part of the boards. Request a breakdown that identifies those pieces. If an owner adds furniture after completion, keep those purchases outside the construction total. An existing deck visible at acquisition does not establish its historical component value from present-day rebuilding costs alone.
Patios|Record paved dimensions and note whether the surface is new construction, an overlay, or replacement of an existing area. Delivery of paving materials may not include excavation, base preparation, or drainage. Ask how those services are recorded in the contract. A patio can surround equipment without becoming part of that equipment's purchase cost. Do not use outdoor seating photographs to infer paving area. Where the paving connects to a structural slab, preserve plans or descriptions showing the boundary between the surfaces.
Driveways|A length and width measurement should distinguish different surfaces and branches of an access route. The driveway may serve multiple buildings or a personally used portion of the parcel. Explain that service pattern before allocating costs. A paving invoice can combine surface repair with culvert installation and new grading. Historic purchase allocations and current construction costs should remain separate evidence streams. If a contractor widens only one section, mark the retained pavement so the asset description does not imply complete replacement.
Walkways|Sketch the route with segments for ordinary paths, steps, ramps, and entrance landings. The connection to the building can be important, so photograph transitions rather than only the middle of the path. A contractor may quote accessibility work as one project that includes door changes and interior flooring. Keep those items distinguished from external paving. Shared routes between several rentals require an allocation explanation. If old surfaces remain beneath a new finish, disclose that condition instead of describing the project as full removal.
Fences|Identify parcel boundaries and any shared-maintenance arrangement. Measure installed lengths by material and include gate openings separately. A safety barrier around a pool may share posts with a boundary fence, while screening attached to a building presents other physical facts. Quotes should distinguish materials, installation, removal, and gates where available. When only one storm-damaged run is replaced, note what remains. Neither the total parcel perimeter nor an aerial photograph necessarily establishes the actual quantity owned and installed by this taxpayer.
Retaining walls|Ask for an explanation of what the wall supports, including whether building loads are involved. Elevation drawings and drainage details can be more useful than a finished facade photograph. A replacement facing or cap may leave the principal wall and foundations intact. Record that limited scope. Contractor costs can include excavation, backfill, drainage, and stabilization beyond the visible masonry. Keep the corresponding work descriptions together. Do not infer the complete wall's cost or classification from the price of decorative blocks alone.
Landscaping|Planting plans can establish quantities and species, while invoices show whether the expenditure involved installation or continuing care. Identify areas that serve owner-only space or common property. Landscape companies sometimes group grading, mulch replacement, lighting, irrigation, and mowing in a single statement. Ask for the capital project scope separately from maintenance. Existing mature plantings in an acquired parcel are not equivalent to later nursery purchases. Preserve the acquisition evidence and explain any reconstruction method used to estimate installed improvement costs.
Irrigation systems|Zone maps help show where water is distributed and which property areas benefit. Label valves and the controller, and identify any pump or storage equipment. Trenching records may be needed because current surface photographs cannot show buried line quantities. A controller replacement can leave nearly all of the original system intact. Conversely, additional zones may require new line construction without replacing the controller. Explain those events individually so the cost pool and replacement history follow the actual installation rather than one undifferentiated irrigation label.
Landscape lighting|Make a fixture count by location and type. Record whether lights use a dedicated transformer or share the building's electrical distribution. A supplier invoice for fixtures may not include trenching, cable, or controls. Ask for those installation records where material. Fixtures mounted on the house should be distinguished from ground-mounted garden lights. A nighttime photograph can demonstrate location but usually does not show wiring or attachment. Keep a daylight overview and model information with it to make the equipment identifiable.
Septic systems|An approved layout can establish the tank, field, service connections, and any pump or treatment unit. Installation records often contain important quantities hidden after backfilling. Identify each building served and whether later expansion changed capacity. A repair to one pump does not necessarily mean the field was replaced. Keep maintenance and pumping service invoices separate from construction. Where an acquired system has no original invoices, document available permits and inspections without treating their stated capacity as a direct measure of tax basis.
Water wells|The drilling log and installer records can describe depth, casing, pump, tank, and treatment components that photographs cannot fully show. A current water test does not establish construction cost. Identify which buildings and uses receive water and whether the owner holds the relevant rights. Equipment upgrades may reuse the original bore and distribution. Preserve separate invoices for pump replacement and new piping. If the well serves personal and rental areas, make that shared function explicit before any allocation of the analyzed expenditure.
Stormwater drainage|A drainage sketch should show collection points, slopes, routes, and outlets. Identify whether excavation served only the drainage project or also prepared foundations and paving. A concealed drain needs supporting installation evidence, not an assumed length inferred from a property photograph. Foundation waterproofing may appear in the same contract but requires its own description. When repairs clear existing pipes rather than install new ones, preserve that distinction. For shared site systems, describe the buildings or parcels served by the constructed work.
EV charging equipment|Record charger ownership, location, rated capacity, and whether equipment was leased. An electrician's scope should distinguish the branch circuit from a service upgrade supporting several loads. Include utility rebates and other adjustments for preparer review without assuming their treatment. Parking construction and charging hardware need separate factual descriptions. If the equipment was installed after purchase, retain its service date independently from the building. A charge-management subscription is not the same expenditure as the physical station and its installation.
Solar equipment|The installer schedule should identify panels, inverter, racks, batteries, and interconnection work. Match equipment quantities to the actual array and retain documents concerning incentives or credits for basis review. Roof work may have been coordinated with the installation without becoming part of the panel cost. Owned and leased installations need different ownership evidence. Later additions can have separate dates and costs. When reviewing a replacement, distinguish retired equipment from components remaining in the array and keep removal or disposal records.
Backup generators|A load schedule helps show what receives backup power. Identify whether the unit is movable or integrated with the property, and record its platform, fuel connections, transfer equipment, and controls. The same installer may have completed unrelated panel work. Request that distinction rather than attributing all electrical costs to the generator. Service contracts and fuel purchases are different transactions from equipment acquisition. A replacement can reuse substantial supporting infrastructure, so the old and new system descriptions should show those retained elements.
Battery storage|Document the module count and the system's connection to solar equipment, building circuits, or a particular operational load. Installer diagrams can show whether an inverter or control unit was already present. A capacity expansion may involve new modules without replacement of the enclosure. Keep the project chronology and applicable incentive records for preparer review. The nominal storage capacity is not a substitute for cost evidence. Distinguish hardware purchases from software subscriptions and explain whether equipment is owned, rented, or supplied under another arrangement.
Refrigerators|An inventory should state the model, room, transfer history, and whether the unit was bought separately. Delivery, installation, and removal fees may appear on the same appliance invoice. Identify which charges relate to the new purchase. Built-in openings and cabinet changes should remain separately described. A second refrigerator in a shared or personally used space needs location and use information. Seller listing images can show a refrigerator that was excluded from the sale, so verify the final transferred inventory.
Dishwashers|Keep the appliance model and installation charge identifiable. An invoice may cover a standard replacement hookup or broader alterations to water, waste, cabinets, and electrical service. Those scopes should not be conflated. Ask whether the removed dishwasher was included in a previous furnishings or appliance schedule. A kitchen photograph demonstrates placement but not the cost assigned at acquisition. If the supplier issues a rebate or credit, preserve it with the net purchase record so the report does not analyze an outdated gross price.
Ranges and ovens|A range, separate cooktop, wall oven, hood, and associated cabinetry should be identified individually. Converting fuel types can create installation expenses that exceed the appliance's delivery cost. Request the contractor's description of gas, wiring, ventilation, and structural changes. A turnkey acquisition may already allocate cooking equipment through a furnishings schedule. Match that list to the actual transferred items. When replacing only an oven, explain the retained cooktop and cabinet opening rather than describing a complete kitchen-equipment replacement.
Washers and dryers|A stacked set can still contain distinct identifiable units and installation work. Record the model numbers and whether the owner purchased, leased, or acquired the machines with the property. Vent relocation and room plumbing are not established by an appliance-store receipt alone. Keep those contractor scopes separately available. Replacing one machine may leave the other and the entire room setup intact. Laundry used by both guests and the owner should be described as shared rather than assumed to serve only the rental activity.
HVAC systems|A mechanical schedule can show which unit serves each zone and whether ducts or other distribution are shared. Ordinary building temperature control should be distinguished from equipment serving a special operational load. Obtain the functional description from the installer where necessary. A service invoice for repair work is different from an equipment replacement contract. When only a condenser changes, record the retained air handler, ducts, and controls. A report should not describe an entire new system if the project replaced one unit.
Mini-split systems|List every indoor head and its outdoor connection. Different rooms can share a compressor, which should not be counted repeatedly in each room's cost allocation. Installation records may include refrigerant line lengths, mounting, and electrical work not visible in completed photographs. Adding a head can be an expansion while retaining the existing outdoor equipment. State what spaces receive ordinary comfort cooling and identify any special-purpose use separately. The absence of ducts is a construction fact, not by itself a supported depreciation classification.
Water heaters|Specify whether the unit supplies the building's ordinary hot-water needs or a distinct operational system. An installer scope may include a heater replacement, vent modifications, fuel supply work, and extensive distribution repairs. Keep the actual work packages identifiable. A tankless label does not settle ownership, basis, or recovery period. In a multi-unit building, show which apartments share the heater. If a renovation replaces plumbing elsewhere without replacing the heater, the record should not describe the entire hot-water system as new equipment.
Kitchen cabinetry|Cabinet shop drawings can show attachment, dimensions, and integration with counters, sinks, and appliances. Loose storage should be inventoried separately from built-in construction. Descriptions such as custom, specialty, or luxury do not establish a short life. Ask the provider how its technical conclusion relates to the actual cabinet function. A contractor's total can include demolition, wall repair, installation, and countertops. When only doors or hardware change, document that partial scope rather than representing the project as replacement of all cabinetry.
Bathroom vanities|Photograph the cabinet's attachment and the sink connection. A freestanding appearance can still conceal permanently installed plumbing. Mirrors, movable storage, counters, and room finishes may have separate invoices even when purchased as a coordinated design. Identify which items were transferred in the original acquisition and which were later installed. A renovation replacing a vanity cabinet may retain the basin and supply lines. Give the reviewer that chronology so the report does not analyze retained fixtures as new purchases.
Carpeting|Room measurements should identify actual coverage rather than total floor area. Retain the product and installation description, including whether underlay and subfloor work were charged separately. Replacement invoices sometimes combine tear-out, disposal, new covering, and structural repair. Keep these distinctions visible. In an acquisition, current carpet-store prices can be a costing input but are not the buyer's newly incurred basis. If only one room was recarpeted, record the limited area and retained coverings rather than updating the whole property's inventory.
Luxury vinyl flooring|Identify the installation method from manufacturer instructions or the installer, especially for click-lock versus adhesive installations. Photographs of a finished surface may not answer that question. A flooring contract can include leveling, moisture treatment, underlay, and repairs to the structural floor. Describe these layers separately where material. Different rooms may use different products despite similar appearance. When a replacement covers the old surface rather than removes it, disclose that fact so the report and any disposition discussion follow what actually occurred.
Tile flooring|A tile assembly can include adhesive, waterproofing, board, structural substrate, and heating components. The installer can explain which layers were newly constructed and which remained. Photograph progress when available rather than relying solely on the finished decorative pattern. A bathroom renovation may have wall tile and floor tile under the same material invoice. Keep measured areas separate. Replacing a few damaged tiles is a different physical scope from replacing the complete floor assembly, and that distinction should remain visible in the records.
Window treatments|An inventory can identify each window, treatment type, control, and installation method. Loose drapes and removable rods should not be confused with integral shading construction. A motorized package may include a control hub shared among many windows. Avoid counting the shared hub repeatedly. Installer charges may also include wall repair or electrical changes. Retain seller inventory exclusions where treatments were removed before closing. If fabric changes while tracks remain, preserve the retained parts in the replacement description.
Decorative lighting|A fixture schedule should distinguish plug-in lamps from hardwired installations and identify what each fixture illuminates. The decorative design of a chandelier does not by itself explain its tax treatment. An electrician's invoice may include general circuit repairs and controls alongside fixture installation. Request that scope rather than assigning the entire bill to a particular light. Seller furnishings lists sometimes include lamps but exclude installed fixtures. Reconcile that difference and retain the model or product description for material purchased equipment.
Security cameras|Record the camera model, recorder, network connection, and any equipment owned by a monitoring provider. Hardware and subscription services should remain separate lines in the evidence file. Cabling may have been installed earlier and reused during an equipment upgrade. Explain that chronology. For guest-facing properties, collect technical photographs without sharing footage containing identifiable guests. A security vendor's package can combine cameras, alarms, access control, and general building work. Identify the actual systems before the provider proposes component allocations.
Smart locks|Identify the door and access device separately. A lock invoice may include recurring platform charges, an installation kit, and labor modifying the door. Wider door replacement should not be inferred from the lock purchase. A shared hub may operate several devices across one property or portfolio; keep its allocation clear. Document whether older access devices were removed, retained as backups, or transferred elsewhere. Photographs should show installation facts without exposing guest codes, administrator credentials, or security-sensitive access information.
Network equipment|Draw a simple equipment map showing the modem, router, switches, access points, and installed cabling. Clarify which hardware belongs to the owner and which is rented from the service provider. A managed-network invoice may bundle monthly support with installation and equipment. Separate those transactions. Upgrades can reuse cables and racks while replacing only electronic units. An asset inventory should retain model and location so equipment moved between rentals is not mistakenly treated as a newly purchased item at each destination.
Televisions|A room inventory should identify display size, model, serial number where available, mounting, and purchase or transfer history. Wall mounts and installation charges may be separately documented from the screen. A media wall can involve cabinetry, wiring, and structural work beyond the television itself. Keep those scopes visible. If a seller retains a displayed screen or substitutes another before closing, update the acquisition inventory. Moving an existing television between guest rooms changes location rather than establishing a fresh acquisition cost.
Game-room equipment|A game room should be inventoried by item rather than treated as one asset named entertainment package. Delivery and assembly costs can be substantial for a pool table and may appear separately from the purchase invoice. Built-in bars, plumbing, flooring, and room electrical work have their own functions. Identify leased arcade machines or equipment supplied by an operator. When an owner sells one game and buys another, preserve both transaction records and the prior inventory instead of overwriting the equipment history.
Beds and mattresses|Match supplier quantities to the actual guest-room inventory. A mattress, loose frame, attached headboard, and built-in bunk structure can involve distinct physical facts. Shipping and installation charges may cover a group of items and need an explained allocation. A furnishings bill of sale can include used beds without identifying every model, so confirm the transferred count. If an owner replaces mattresses while retaining frames, describe that limited replacement. The capacity advertised in a rental listing is not evidence of purchased quantities or costs.
Sofas and seating|Record loose pieces and modular sections with enough detail to distinguish them from fixed benches. Upholstery repairs may be recorded separately from buying a new item. A seller can retain furnishings shown in marketing photographs, so rely on the final transfer list. Delivery or assembly invoices may cover several rooms. If a sofa is moved from another property, preserve its existing asset identity rather than treating it as a new purchase. Identify salvaged or sold pieces when the guest layout changes.
Dining furniture|An inventory should distinguish a loose table and chairs from seating constructed into the room. Banquettes can have cabinetry, wall attachment, upholstery, and surrounding finish work. A furniture-store invoice may cover only the movable pieces. Show how the complete dining project was acquired or constructed rather than assigning one convenient label. Count chairs actually transferred at closing and note later replacements. A room's maximum guest seating does not establish the number of purchased assets or the portion of building basis attributable to them.
Outdoor furniture|List movable chairs, tables, umbrellas, bases, storage boxes, and installed seating separately. Seasonal storage can explain why acquisition photographs and current inventory differ. Shipping costs may cover furniture delivered to several rentals, requiring a documented allocation. A hardscape contractor may build fixed seating without supplying loose furniture. Keep those acquisitions distinct. If pieces were transferred from another property, retain their original cost and history. Sun damage and later replacement should be documented by actual removed items rather than a generic patio allowance.
Sheds and storage buildings|A site plan should locate the structure and explain its foundations, utilities, dimensions, and use. A preassembled product can still be installed as a building, so portability needs actual evidence. Shelving and loose storage equipment inside may have separate invoices and ownership. If the owner stores personal items alongside rental supplies, disclose that use. A current shed photograph cannot establish its original cost or the extent of installation. Replacement work may reuse a slab or utilities that should not be treated as newly constructed.
Garage doors and openers|The installer can distinguish panels, tracks, springs, motor equipment, controls, and changes to the structural opening. Request that description where the invoice only says garage package. The report should describe what serves the building and what was actually replaced. A new opener can use retained rails or controls, while a damaged door repair can leave the motor intact. Keep the old system identity and any sale or removal evidence. A warranty replacement or credit may change the net cost recorded for new equipment.
Appliance-dedicated electrical circuits|Circuit labels and electrician notes can show which outlet or branch serves a specific appliance. The broader distribution panel may serve the entire building and other renovation work. Ask for the line route, equipment served, and distinction between new branch installation and shared service changes. Do not infer dedicated use merely because an outlet happens to be near an appliance. If the appliance and wiring were acquired together, the study needs a supported allocation within the existing basis rather than added hypothetical installation costs.
