A cost segregation study is worth considering when a supported timing benefit justifies the study and implementation costs for your facts. A property-value threshold, a tax bracket or an impressive example cannot make that decision alone. The right next step may be to proceed, wait for better records or decline.

1. Your usable benefit is uncertain

Ask your preparer to compare depreciation with and without the proposed study and identify how much of the additional deduction could be used in each period. If a loss is limited, its possible future value needs a separate analysis. A gross deduction multiplied by a marginal tax rate is not a reliable current-year benefit when the deduction cannot be used now.

A short-stay label does not resolve the owner’s activity and participation facts. Complete that review before relying on a property estimate to justify the fee.

2. You expect to sell or exchange soon

Have the adviser model the sale by asset, using adjusted basis, depreciation and proposed sale allocations. Recapture is not universally a flat 25% tax on every depreciation dollar. Section 1245 ordinary-income recapture and unrecaptured section 1250 gain are different calculations. Their application depends on the assets and transaction.

A planned 1031 exchange does not automatically defer every component’s tax consequences. Current like-kind exchange nonrecognition generally applies to qualifying real property; asset classification and recapture rules still need review. Do not commission a study on the assumption that an exchange erases every later consequence. Prepare the sale and recapture review.

3. The property cost file cannot support the proposed analysis

Pause when the closing statement, land allocation, ownership record or earlier schedule is unresolved. Ask what alternative evidence could be acceptable, who must provide it and how uncertainty would appear in the report. A larger estimate is not a remedy for a missing source.

For a furnished purchase, reconcile seller-transferred items and later invoices first. For renovations, request final scopes and credits rather than using the original budget. Use the missing-document workflow.

4. The complete cost exceeds the expected value

Stratum pricing is $1 per square foot with a $2,000 minimum per property. Add separately engaged return implementation and advisory costs when evaluating the engagement. A small rental is not automatically unsuitable, and a large purchase is not automatically worthwhile. Supported depreciable costs, deduction timing and your holding period matter more than an arbitrary purchase-price cutoff.

Illustrative fee-only comparison: a 1,400-square-foot property and a 1,900-square-foot property each meet the $2,000 minimum. Their fees are equal, but their economics may be very different. This example supplies no deduction or savings assumption.

5. Use or ownership needs to be resolved first

A wholly personal property is not a rental study candidate. Mixed rental and personal use needs preparer review; it is not always a simple percentage applied to every deduction. If you lease the home, distinguish the landlord’s property from your furnishings and improvements. Resolve those boundaries before commissioning a building-wide study.

Record a proceed, wait or decline decision

  • Proceed: the scope and cost file are clear, and the preparer has evaluated usable benefit.
  • Wait: a missing record, participation review, ownership question or planned transaction could change the decision.
  • Decline: the supported economics do not justify the full engagement for your situation.

Use the study decision guide and the fee-versus-assumed-benefit worksheet. Keep the assumptions, responsible adviser and review date with the decision so a later change can be evaluated.
