Read a cost segregation report as a chain from the property and cost records to the proposed component classifications and the preparer’s implementation. The first-year deduction is only one output. Ask what supports each material conclusion and whether the report describes the assignment you commissioned.

1. Confirm the property and version

Check the legal owner, address, unit or parcel, relevant dates, study scope and report version. Compare the scope with the signed engagement. Identify excluded buildings, furnishings or later improvements. If you receive a correction, retain the superseded version with an explanation and make sure the preparer uses the accepted version.

2. Follow the cost reconciliation

The analyzed pool should connect to supported acquisition or construction costs, with land, separately recorded items and other adjustments explained. Ask for a written bridge when the report, books and closing statement show different amounts. A reconciled total is necessary arithmetic; it does not independently prove an allocation is correct.

Fictional arithmetic example: a $600,000 purchase less a supported $120,000 land allocation leaves $480,000 before other adjustments. If $20,000 of that remaining amount is separately identified as transferred furniture, the report must show how the furniture and remaining building allocation fit within the same $480,000 pool. Adding the furniture again would double-count it. These assumed amounts do not establish your land value, asset classes or deduction.

3. Trace material components to evidence

Ask how a component identifier connects to its description, location, cost source and classification reasoning. The IRS audit guide discusses documentation, methodology and cost reconciliation. It is an examination reference, not a guarantee of acceptance or an automatic pass/fail certification.

A label such as specialty electrical should identify what the work actually serves. An estimate should explain its support and limitations. When a conclusion looks inconsistent with your property, ask for clarification; do not silently revise the schedule yourself.

4. Separate classifications from the depreciation calculation

Confirm the depreciation system, recovery period, method, convention, service dates and applicable elections with the preparer. Current bonus depreciation is not simply an election to claim the same result on every component: eligibility and acquisition history matter, and an election out may apply. State treatment needs its own review. Use the bonus-depreciation checklist rather than treating the report year as the controlling date.

5. Identify questions before the return is implemented

  • Does the final component schedule reconcile with the report summary?
  • Were seller-transferred furnishings counted once and later purchases separated?
  • Are unknown dates, missing records and inaccessible areas identified?
  • Which professional determines the filing procedure and handles earlier depreciation?
  • What is the process for corrections and technical follow-up?

Download the blank report acceptance and CPA handoff register. The register records questions and completion evidence; it does not approve a report or prepare a return.

6. Keep the report useful after this year

Retain the component identifiers as items are replaced, moved or sold. Connect new invoices and disposition records to the prior schedule. Before sale, give your adviser the original report and current asset history, not just a summary of the original deductions. Prepare the sale review when plans change.
