Five-, seven- and fifteen-year recovery periods are common topics in cost segregation, but an item’s name alone does not determine its class. These educational examples help STR and Airbnb owners prepare evidence. They are not drawn from verified client studies and do not predict a reclassification percentage or deduction.

Five-year examples need the right activity context

Publication 527 identifies appliances, carpeting and furniture used in a residential rental real estate activity as five-year GDS property. That guidance does not mean every fixture or installation inside a rental belongs in that class. Provide the actual use, ownership and cost records and have the applicable system confirmed.

A sofa acquired with a furnished home, a later replacement sofa and a manager-owned sofa need different ownership and cost histories. Use the furnishings guide to distinguish them before making a classification request.

Seven-year treatment is not a default for every furnishing

Publication 527 separately lists office furniture in the seven-year GDS class. Ask which asset class and activity apply to the actual item. A marketing description such as luxury furniture or business equipment is not a tax classification. A room used as an office does not dispense with reviewing ownership, function and the relevant rules.

Fifteen-year property still requires a supported conclusion

Some land improvements and qualifying interior improvements to nonresidential property can have fifteen-year treatment under the applicable rules. A deck, pool, site utility or retaining wall should not be assigned a period solely because it is outdoors. Qualified improvement property has specific conditions and exclusions; a residential remodel is not automatically QIP.

For mixed invoices, distinguish equipment, structures, building systems and site work. Give the reviewer installation detail and supported cost allocations rather than a preferred short-life label.

A recovery-period example is not a bonus-depreciation conclusion

Even where a component has a short recovery period, the preparer still needs its acquisition history, service date, depreciation system and applicable elections. Current bonus rules should be evaluated separately. State deductions can differ from federal treatment. Review the dated bonus checklist.

Keep sale treatment separate as well

A recovery-period label alone does not settle section 1245 or section 1250 treatment, recognized gain or recapture. Preserve the component schedule and replacement history for the adviser handling a sale. Do not use the class label as a shortcut to a universal exit tax rate.

Questions to ask about your report

  • Which actual asset and function does each row describe?
  • What source establishes the allocated cost?
  • Which authority and activity support the proposed class?
  • Does the schedule reconcile to the approved cost pool?
  • Which eligibility, state or sale questions remain for the preparer?
