# Is Cost Segregation Worth It for an Airbnb Under $500,000?

An Airbnb's purchase price does not, by itself, establish whether a cost segregation study is worthwhile. A smaller property can deserve evaluation, but it can also have limited usable benefit, a short expected hold or incomplete records that make the full engagement cost difficult to justify. The answer should come from the property's facts rather than a universal minimum price or promised ROI multiple.

For a lower-cost rental, the study fee and implementation work can represent a meaningful share of the expected benefit. That makes a careful comparison more important, not less. A large advertised deduction percentage is not a substitute for an estimate of when the owner can actually use the resulting depreciation.

This guide explains how to review a sub-$500,000 STR without assuming that every property above a particular basis threshold should order a study.

## Purchase price, value and depreciable basis are different inputs

Start by labeling the amount being discussed. A contract price, appraisal, mortgage balance and approved depreciable basis can be different numbers. An estimate should identify which one it uses and what remains unresolved.

Land is not depreciable. Separately acquired furnishings, closing adjustments, improvements and prior history can also affect the cost pool. A report that applies a percentage to the entire listing value can look more favorable than a projection built from an approved basis reconciliation.

Read the [land allocation guide](/blog/land-value-allocation-cost-segregation/) and [mortgage FAQ](/blog/can-you-do-cost-segregation-airbnb-mortgage/) before comparing estimates. Borrowing more does not automatically enlarge the underlying property cost or establish a usable deduction.

## Separate a deduction estimate from a cash benefit

A study may identify supported depreciation, while owner-level rules delay some or all of its tax effect. Ask the preparer to review activity treatment, participation and applicable limitations before multiplying a proposed deduction by a marginal rate.

The timing matters especially when the fee is paid now but the benefit is deferred. A carryover may have value, but it should not be presented as the same thing as a current refund. State treatment and later sale consequences also belong in the review.

Use the [passive loss guide](/blog/passive-activity-loss-rules-cost-segregation/) and [excess business loss review](/blog/excess-business-loss-limitation-cost-segregation/) to identify unanswered questions. The study provider's component analysis and the preparer's usability conclusion are separate deliverables.

## Understand the actual fee structure

Stratum studies cost $1 per square foot, with a $2,000 minimum per property. Under that published rule, a 1,100-square-foot property reaches the $2,000 minimum, while a 2,200-square-foot property has a $2,200 study fee. Confirm the square footage and engagement scope rather than assuming purchase price determines the fee.

Two separate 1,000-square-foot properties should not be treated as one $2,000 engagement merely by adding their square footage. The minimum is per property. Confirm any written portfolio arrangement before including a discount in the model; do not assume one exists.

Tax advice, method-change preparation and return implementation may involve separate costs. A comparison that subtracts only the study fee can overstate the net benefit if material filing work is also required.

## A hypothetical usable-benefit comparison

Assume a preparer-supported projection shows $5,000 of earlier tax benefit for a particular scenario. Assume a $2,000 study fee and $800 of separate implementation cost. The simple initial difference is $2,200: $5,000 minus $2,000 minus $800.

These are fictional economic inputs, not an estimate for a property of any stated purchase price. The example does not establish the underlying deduction, bonus eligibility, future sale treatment or a lifetime net return. It shows why fees and usable benefit should be stated in the same model.

If the projected current benefit were zero because the relevant loss was deferred, the immediate cash comparison would look different even if a future benefit remained possible. If the owner sold sooner than assumed, the exit analysis could also change the result. A useful estimate explains these conditions instead of promising a fixed multiple of the fee.

## Why two similarly priced Airbnbs can have different answers

The properties can differ in land allocation, acquired assets, improvement history, use and documentation. Their owners can differ in income, participation, carryovers and expected holding period. Similar contract prices do not establish similar reclassification percentages or identical tax savings.

One owner may have a clear furnishing inventory and usable deductions, while another has uncertain acquisition records and substantial personal use. A percentage estimate that ignores those differences can conceal the very facts that determine whether the engagement makes sense.

The [vacation-rental guide](/blog/cost-segregation-vacation-rentals/) and [when-to-wait checklist](/blog/when-not-to-do-cost-segregation/) help identify those decision points. A supported decision to postpone a study is more useful than an unsupported positive projection.

## Existing properties need an implementation review

A smaller rental purchased in an earlier year may still be worth evaluating. The preparer needs the prior depreciation schedules, original dates, elections, improvements and disposals before determining the appropriate filing route.

Do not assume that a new report automatically recovers every missed amount in the current year or that the owner can choose any convenient year for a catch-up deduction. The procedure and the resulting usability need separate review.

Use the [existing-property guide](/blog/cost-segregation-existing-property/) and [Form 3115 review](/blog/form-3115-look-back-cost-segregation/) to prepare that discussion. Include the additional implementation work in the cost comparison rather than treating the study as the only project expense.

## Small-property cost segregation FAQ

### Is $500,000 a legal minimum for a study?

It is not a universal eligibility threshold described by the depreciation framework. A provider may have its own engagement criteria, but the owner's economic decision still depends on supported costs, applicable treatment, usable benefits and fees.

### Is a $150,000 depreciable basis enough to guarantee ROI?

No. A basis figure does not establish the component results, owner-level usability, implementation costs or exit consequences. Avoid replacing one unsupported price threshold with another.

### Does a higher tax bracket make every study worthwhile?

No. The proposed deduction may be limited or deferred, and fees and sale effects still matter. A marginal-rate multiplication is not a complete analysis of the owner's return.

### Can separately purchased furniture be handled without a building study?

Ask the preparer how known assets should be recorded. A study may address a broader property allocation, while a clear furniture purchase can have its own evidence and treatment. Confirm scope so the same items are not counted twice.

### Will several small properties produce a proportionate portfolio benefit?

Not automatically. Review each property's costs, history and fee, then coordinate the taxpayer-level limitations. Do not add several theoretical property deductions and call the total current tax savings.

### Does selling soon make the study pointless?

Not universally, but it changes the comparison. The [short-hold sale guide](/blog/cost-segregation-selling-property-early/) explains why usable deductions, recapture, timing and full costs need to be modeled together.

### Should I choose the provider with the largest percentage estimate?

Compare the assumptions and evidence first. Different starting bases, scope and usability assumptions can make preliminary estimates incomparable. Use the [proposal checklist](/blog/str-cost-segregation-proposal-comparison/) to normalize the inputs.

## Decide what information would change the answer

Before ordering work, list the unresolved facts that could materially change the economics. These might include the land allocation, personal use, prior schedules, expected sale date or the preparer's loss-usability conclusion. Resolve the most important ones before requesting greater numerical precision.

Then document the accepted scenario: property cost pool, study scope, applicable treatment, timing of usable benefit, fees and exit assumptions. Keep an alternative scenario for a delayed benefit or shorter hold. This makes the decision more transparent than a generic “five times ROI” claim.

The [ROI checklist](/blog/roi-cost-segregation-study/) provides a broader framework. Bring the property records and open questions to a [discovery call](/cost-seg-discovery/) and confirm the written engagement and separate filing responsibilities.

## Ask what the estimate leaves out

A useful preliminary estimate should identify exclusions as well as potential benefits. Ask whether it assumes an approved land allocation, complete prior schedules, no personal use or a particular filing procedure. Clarify which of those inputs are confirmed and which require further review.

Then ask how the estimate would change if a key assumption fails. A delayed deduction, additional implementation fee or earlier sale should not be hidden in general fine print. Showing the sensitivity can help an owner decide whether to gather more records before ordering the study.

Keep the response with the proposal and the preparer's analysis. If two providers produce very different numbers, first reconcile their starting inputs and scope. A larger estimate may reflect a different basis or unsupported usability assumption rather than a better result.

For a smaller property, this comparison is particularly useful because modest fee or timing differences can materially affect the decision. The goal is a supported answer to whether this engagement fits this owner, not a claim that every property in a broad price band should proceed.
